Credit risk mitigation refers to mechanisms to ameliorate the exposure of financial 
institutions to counterparty default. Three common approaches used are netting, 
collateralisation and usage of a central clearing counterparty. 
Netting refers to the idea that we can reduce credit exposure by aggregating multiple bilateral 
transactions with the same counterparty into a single net obligation, such that instead of each 
contract being settled individually, where gains and losses across contracts are offset. This 
reduces the total exposure as the net m2m amount is a risk if the counterparty defaults. In 
practice, such netting agreements are implemented an regulated via ISDA master agreements. 
This is because, this can come from swaps, and if we are on the OTM leg, that is a liability, 
and is counted against us, whereas we are ITM, and the counterparty defaults, we have made 
a loss. The idea is that by netting, we can narrow such incidences.  
As a result, it lowers current exposures, expected exposures, expected losses and CV A. 
However, netting introduces legal risk, since the enforceability of the netting agreement must 
be recognised under the relevant jurisdiction at the time of default, especially since many 
agreements are cross-border, and is subject to local regulations. It needs to be also noted that 
netting effectively changes the seniority structure of debt, and in that sense, is a transfer of 
risk from counterparty to bondholders. 
 
Collateralisation, as its names suggest is obvious, is under the ISDA Credit Support Annex, 
which reduces credit risk by requiring counterparty with a negative mark-to-market position 
(or not having enough maintenance margin etc.) to post collateral, subject to a minimum 
transfer amount, This collateral serves as protection against losses if the counterparty 
defaults. Collateral is typically exchanged through variation margin, which adjusts daily 
according to market movements, and sometimes initial margin, which protects against 
potential future exposures. 
Collateralisation, like the above, can reduce exposures. But it does introduce liquidity risk, 
since the counterparties must meet the margin calls immediately. Operational risks will also 
persist, since it is complex. Gap risk, can arise because exposures naturally can still change 
between margin calls.  
Notably, collateralisation does not reduce risk to 0, and exposure is still equal to the 
thresholds plus movement in the market value over the period, and potentially the MTA itself, 


and there is still residual exposure. The collateral may also be insufficient with huge 
volatilies. 
 
The use of clearing houses, as third parties that stand between the original transacting parties, 
was mandated after the GFC. After clearing, each original party faces the CCP rather than 
each other. The goal is to improve the market, by having greater transparency, 
standardization, and the ability of the CCP to apply risk mitigation tools centrally, rather than 
having all the derivatives traded OTC. 
The idea is the note that the risk is not eliminated, and while trades are balanced, so that the 
CCP has no ordinary market risk before default, it still bears the default risk. To manage this, 
CCPs themselves, have collateralisation, netting, default settlement rules and clearing house 
capital. In the cause of defaults, there are management tools such as auctioning off trades, 
reassigning trades, variation margin gains haircut, and tear-ups. 
As such, the use of CCPs reduces bilateral counterparty risk, but centralises the risk. IF the 
margins and other protections are insufficient, the losses can arise the clearing house level.  
Cumulatively, all three mitigants reduce counterparty risk, but none of them makes risk 
disappear, each with their own idiosyncrasies. It mitigates but does not eliminate risks 
whatsoever.
